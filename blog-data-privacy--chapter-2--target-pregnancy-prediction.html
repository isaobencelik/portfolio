When the model works, and that's the problem

08 October 2026 · 2 min read

Most data scandals we remember are breaches: someone broke in and took something. One of the cases in my Data Privacy, Security and Ethics course at NOVA IMS is uncomfortable for a different reason. Nothing was stolen. The system worked exactly as designed.

What happened

In 2012, Target used predictive analytics to identify customers who might be pregnant, based on their purchasing patterns. The goal was marketing: reach people at a moment when their shopping habits are about to change.

The model worked. In one case, it revealed a teenager's pregnancy to her father before she had told him.

Why it's a data ethics case

The course frames the problem as a lack of privacy considerations. The purpose was marketing, but it still affected a customer's life.

That's the part I find most useful. There was no hacker, no leaked database and no broken law in the story as it's usually told. The harm came from an accurate prediction landing in the wrong place.

It connects to principles the course lists for data ethics: privacy, respecting individuals' privacy; transparency, people's right to know how their data will be used; and avoidance of harm, making sure data practices don't hurt individuals or communities, including by thinking through the negative consequences in advance.

Accuracy is the wrong finish line

When teams review a model, the conversation is usually about performance: precision, recall, lift. Those matter. But this case shows they aren't the whole test.

Two extra questions would have caught it:

  • What does this output reveal? A prediction can be more sensitive than any of the data that went into it. Shopping baskets aren't medical records. A pregnancy prediction effectively is.
  • Who will see it? A score sitting in a database is one thing. A coupon in a family's post box is another. The channel that delivers the output is part of the privacy decision.

Where I'd use this

In product work, I'd add these two questions to any review of a feature that predicts something about a person: recommendations, segments, risk scores, personalised messages.

A few practical checks follow from them:

  • Classify the output, not just the input. If the prediction would be sensitive data if someone had typed it in, treat it that way.
  • Look at the delivery channel. Who else could see the result: family members, colleagues, anyone on a shared device?
  • Ask whether the person would expect it. If someone would be surprised that you know this about them, that's a signal to stop and think.

The bigger lesson

It's easy to think of privacy as a security problem: lock the data down and you're done. This case shows that privacy is also a design problem. The most careful security in the world doesn't help if the product itself is built to reveal something people never chose to share.

A prediction looks harmless in a backlog. It looks very different on a family's kitchen table.
